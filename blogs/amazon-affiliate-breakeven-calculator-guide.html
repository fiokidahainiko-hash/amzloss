Amazon Affiliate Break-Even Calculator Guide

⚖️Free tool:Break-Even Calculator — find the price point that actually pays.

Most affiliate math stops at one question: "What does this product pay per sale?" That number can look great on paper and still lose you money. The question that actually decides whether a product is worth your time is different: how many sales — or how much commission — does this page need to earn to pay back the hours you spent writing it? That is break-even, and in 2026, with rates squeezed and reporting thinner than ever, it is the number that separates busy affiliates from profitable ones.

What break-even actually means for an affiliate

Break-even is the point where a page's projected commission equals the cost of producing it. If a full review with comparison tables and photos takes you six hours and you value that time at $25 an hour, the page costs $150 before it earns a cent. A product paying $3 per sale needs 50 sales just to stand still. A product paying $12 per sale needs just over 12. Same effort, wildly different requirements — and the difference shows up before you write a single word if you run the numbers first.

This is why the commission rate alone is misleading. A 10% rate on a $20 product earns $2 a sale. A 6% rate on a $300 product earns $18 a sale. The Commission Calculator gives you the per-sale number; break-even tells you what that number has to do to justify the page.

How the break-even calculator works

The Break-Even Calculator turns this reasoning into a concrete answer. You enter:

  • The product price and its category rate in your marketplace.
  • Your expected click-through and conversion rates — how many readers click your link and how many of those actually buy.
  • The hours a page takes to research, write, and maintain.

The tool then returns the number of sales required to cover your time, the projected monthly commission at your current traffic, and a clear verdict: write a dedicated review, fold the product into a roundup, or skip it. It also shows what happens when you change one variable — bumping conversion from 2% to 4% often flips a page from losing to profitable.

The variables that quietly decide everything

  • Conversion rate: a $40 item that converts at 3% can out-earn a $400 item that converts at 0.3%. Break-even favors products your audience actually buys.
  • Return rate: commission is clawed back when an order is returned. If a category bounces 15% of purchases, discount your expected commission by that much before you celebrate.
  • Volume: cheap products can break even, but only at scale. One roundup covering ten cheap items has the same time cost as one review and ten chances to convert.

When a page crosses break-even, the next question is whether you're being paid what the math expects. That's what the Earnings Audit checks on your Product Earnings Report — it flags every line paid below the correct rate, so a short payout doesn't quietly turn a profitable page into a losing one.

Run break-even before you write, not after

The cheapest mistake in affiliate publishing is writing the page and discovering the numbers later. Decide with the calculator, write with confidence, and re-check whenever Amazon changes a rate or you change a template's conversion. Break-even is a moving target — the tool makes it a number you can see, not a guess.

Frequently asked questions

What is break-even in affiliate marketing?

Break-even is the point where the commission a product earns you equals the cost of the time you spent creating content about it. Below that point you are subsidizing Amazon with your effort; above it, the page starts earning profit.

How do I use the break-even calculator?

Enter the product price, its category rate, your expected click-through and conversion rates, and the hours a full review takes. The calculator returns the number of sales needed to cover your time, the projected monthly commission, and whether the product is worth a dedicated page.

Should I write about cheap products at all?

Sometimes, but only when the math supports it. A $15 product at 4% earns around 60 cents a sale, so it needs heavy volume or a bundle of related cheap items on one page to cross break-even. Run the numbers before you write rather than after.
