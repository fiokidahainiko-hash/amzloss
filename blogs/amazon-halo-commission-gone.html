Amazon Halo Commission Is Gone (April 14): What It Costs You

📊Free tool:Current Rate Table — see the live 2026 rates by category and market.

On April 14, 2026, Amazon made one of the most consequential changes to the Associates program in years — and most affiliates found out only when their earnings shrank. The "halo commission" is gone: Amazon now pays only on the exact product you linked, not on the other items a reader happened to add to their cart in that session.

This is confirmed on Amazon's own Associates Central "What's Changed" page, effective April 14, 2026. It's separate from the separately-reported rate-card cuts of up to 50% that Amazon has not publicly confirmed. This guide explains what changed, what it means for your earnings, and how to measure the real impact on your own account.

What halo commission was

Before April 14, when a reader clicked your link and bought anything in that session, you earned commission on the qualifying items they bought — including products you never mentioned or linked. It was the quiet upside of Amazon affiliate marketing: link a $30 kettle, and if the same reader added a $200 espresso machine to the cart, you earned on both.

It was invisible, it padded nearly every click, and it made your real earnings per click higher than the category rate alone suggested. That's why so many people never noticed it was happening — and why so many didn't realize the day it stopped.

What changed on April 14, 2026

Amazon's Operating Agreement update changed the calculation of onsite commission income to apply only to Direct Qualifying Purchases of the same ASIN variant as the linked product detail page. In plain terms:

  • You now earn commission only on the exact product (or its direct variants) you linked.
  • If a reader clicks your kettle link and buys a toaster instead, that toaster earns you nothing.
  • Items added to the cart that you didn't link no longer count toward your commission.

The same update made three other changes worth knowing about:

  • 180-day fulfillment deadline. Products must be shipped, streamed or downloaded, and paid for within 180 days of the click to qualify for commission. This is not a longer cookie — Amazon's cookie is still 24 hours with 90-day cart carryover. It's a settlement deadline, and it puts pre-orders at risk.
  • Paid and boosted ads disqualified. Purchases referred through paid or boosted advertisements linking to Amazon no longer qualify, with limited exceptions.
  • Original content requirement. Amazon added a definition requiring linked pages to contain original commentary, analysis, or transformation — thin product lists and copied specs are weaker.

Why this hurts even if your rate never changed

This is the trap: the halo change can cut your earnings without any visible rate change. Your category rate can look identical and your payout can still fall, because the "extra" items you never linked no longer pay. Publishers have reported income drops around 25% in the months after the change, with the loss concentrated precisely in the cart-wide income that never appeared on any rate table.

That's why checking a rate table isn't enough. The halo loss only shows up in your actual transaction data — as a line item whose reported commission is lower than what the rate on that order's date should have produced.

How to check the impact on your own account

The reliable way to quantify the halo change is to compare what you were actually paid against what the published rate should have produced for your order dates:

  1. Export your data. In Amazon Associates Central, go to Reports → Product Earnings Report and download the CSV for the period around and after April 14.
  2. Run an earnings audit. Our free earnings audit parses the file entirely in your browser, compares each line against the correct rate, and flags every line where the paid commission is below what the rate should produce.
  3. Look at the before/after. Compare flagged amounts for periods before April 14 against periods after. A jump in flagged lines after the change date is the halo loss showing up in your numbers.

How to adapt your content strategy

  1. Link with intent, not volume. Since only the linked ASIN pays, stop relying on generic "shop this store" links. Match each link to the exact product your content is genuinely recommending.
  2. Prioritise the exact-match cart. A reader who buys exactly what you recommended is now your only bet. That rewards honest, specific reviews over blanket product-grid pages.
  3. Add original commentary. With Amazon's new original-content standard, add your own tests, comparisons, and decision rules to every product page.
  4. Re-check your category rates. The halo padding made low-rate categories viable before. Without it, a 1–3% category may no longer justify the SEO effort. See the current rate table for what each category now pays.

Frequently asked questions

What is Amazon halo commission?

Halo commission was the commission Amazon paid on other qualifying items a referred reader bought in the same session, beyond the product you linked. It ended April 14, 2026.

Does Amazon Associates still pay on the rest of the cart?

No. Since April 14, 2026, onsite commission applies only to Direct Qualifying Purchases of the same ASIN variant as the linked product detail page.

Does Amazon Associates have a 180-day cookie?

No. The click window is 24 hours with a 90-day cart carryover. The 180-day figure is a deadline for products to ship, stream or download and be paid for — not extra time to shop.

Were these changes announced?

The Operating Agreement update was published on Amazon's "What's Changed" page. The separate reported rate-card cuts of up to 50% were reported by Adweek and other outlets but have not been publicly confirmed by Amazon.

How do I check the halo change's impact?

Download your Product Earnings Report CSV and audit it — compare reported commission against the expected rate per line, especially across the April 14 boundary. An AmzLoss audit does this in your browser.
