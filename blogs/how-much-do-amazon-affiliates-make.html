How Much Do Amazon Affiliates Make? 2026 Math

🧮Free tool:Amazon Commission Calculator — calculate what any Amazon sale earns.

"How much do Amazon affiliates make?" gets asked more than almost anything else in this space — and it gets answered with a single number that doesn't exist. The truth is that earnings sit on a spectrum defined by two things you control: how many buyers reach your content, and what kind of products those buyers end up buying. In 2026, the second variable changed dramatically. This guide lays out realistic ranges by traffic level and product mix, explains why published averages mislead you, and shows how to run the actual numbers on your own site.

Why the averages you see online mislead you

Most "average affiliate income" figures you'll find are either marketing bait or drawn from self-reported surveys that skew optimistic. A few problems make them useless:

  • Survivor bias. The affiliates still blogging to report their income are the ones making money. The thousands who quit quietly don't appear in the data.
  • Category mixing. A site selling $500 furniture at a 4% rate and a site selling $25 kitchen gadgets at 6% have completely different per-visitor economics, even at identical traffic.
  • Pre-2026 math. Most published "how much can you make" numbers were calculated before the 2026 commission cuts. In the hardest-hit categories, the same traffic now earns roughly half what it did.

So instead of a fake average, work with ranges tied to traffic volume and product price. The same approach our commission calculator uses: price, category, market, and volume in — expected commission out.

Realistic income ranges by traffic level

These are planning ranges for a site publishing genuine buying-intent content, converting at a typical 1% click-through and 5–7% purchase rate. They assume a blended commission of about 5% across categories after the 2026 cuts — some niches will do better, some worse.

  • 0–1,000 visitors/month: roughly $10–$60/month. Useful as validation that your content converts, not as income.
  • 1,000–10,000 visitors/month: roughly $60–$600/month. This is where a serious niche site starts to pay for hosting and tools.
  • 10,000–50,000 visitors/month: roughly $600–$3,000/month. A steady side income for most people, and the minimum range for many to consider going full-time.
  • 50,000+ visitors/month: roughly $3,000–$15,000+/month. Full-time territory — and where commissions start to matter a lot in absolute terms.

Note the deliberate fuzziness. The real driver is average order value. The formula behind all of this is simply: visitors × click-through rate × purchase rate × average order value × blended commission rate. A site with 20,000 visitors but a $400 average order can out-earn one with 80,000 visitors buying $15 gadgets. Run both through the current rate table and the difference becomes obvious fast.

Which levers move your income the most

  • Average order value: the highest-leverage variable you control. A high-ticket category with a mediocre rate usually beats a low-ticket category with a great rate.
  • Purchase intent: comparison, "best of," and "should I buy" content converts far better than informational content. Traffic quality beats traffic quantity.
  • Rate retention after 2026: categories that held their rates are worth more per click than they were before the cuts. The full picture is in the current rate table, and any lingering questions about the cut itself are covered in the FAQ.

Frequently asked questions

Can you make a full-time income as an Amazon affiliate?

Yes, but it typically requires a substantial, consistent audience. Earning $3,000 or more per month usually means tens of thousands of targeted monthly visitors and a product mix of mid-to-high-priced items.

How many visitors do you need to earn $1,000/month?

Plan on roughly 10,000 to 15,000 targeted monthly visitors at a typical 1% click-through and 6% purchase rate. Higher-priced niches need far less traffic.

Do the 2026 commission cuts change the numbers?

Yes. Physical-goods and luxury categories were hit hardest, so the same traffic earns less in those niches. Lean on categories that held their rates and higher-priced items.
